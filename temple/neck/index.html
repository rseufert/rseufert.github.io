What it is

The neck is drawn as it is built, nearly: each fret a twelfth root of two nearer the bridge than the last, so that the twelfth fret halves the string and sounds its octave, though the spacing here is evened out a little so the high frets have room for their names. The notes are equal-tempered from A at 440, and the sound is a plucked string made the way Karplus and Strong made one in 1983, a burst of noise fed round a loop as long as the note's period and softened each time round. The shapes are found by the page, not looked up: in each four-fret window it tries every way of putting a chord tone or nothing under each string, keeps those with the root in the bass, every tone present (a fifth may go missing from a chord of four or more), no muted string above a sounding one and no more than four fingers, a barre counting as one, and shows the most playable at each position. With a capo the capo is the nut, and the shapes are written from it, as a player writes them.

Name a chord turns it round: put a finger on each string you want, by tapping, and the page tries each note you have put down as a root against every chord it knows, the fifth allowed to be missing, and names what it finds, with the lowest note after a slash when it is not the root. Tune by ear asks for the microphone and finds the note's period in what it hears by de Cheveigné and Kawahara's Yin method (2002), says how many cents it is off and which string it is nearest; the sound is looked at as it comes in and kept nowhere. The address carries the whole setting, the key, the scale, the chord and its root, the tuning and the capo, so a link sends someone the neck as you have it; the cards and the wheel send a trump's or a sign's note here the same way.

A tab is read the way it is written: each run of lines of dashes is a line of music, a line to a string with the highest on top, and the letters in front of them are the tuning; a header naming Drop D or DADGAD, or a capo, is taken at its word, and chord names written over the lines are kept. A plain tab has no rhythm, only order and spacing, so the page plays it by its columns, so many a second, a bar line taking no time, and the speed is yours. A note after an h, a p or a slide is played softer, as a hammer or a slide sounds, and a new note on a string stops the last one there. The key is guessed by Krumhansl and Schmuckler's method, weighing how often each note sounds against the profiles of the major and minor keys, and the neck lights that scale behind the tab. Greensleeves is arranged here from the traditional tune. A tab you open stays in this browser; keep it under a name to have it next time.

The tone scale is Paul Foster Case's, from the Builders of the Adytum's tarot of the 1920s, which hung a note and a colour on each of the twenty-two trumps. Laid out it is simpler than it sounds: the twelve semitones from C go round the zodiac from Aries, as the twelve colours go round the spectrum from red, so that C is Aries and the Emperor, and red; E is Leo and Strength, and yellow; B is Pisces and the Moon, and violet-red. Each planet and element sits on the note of its trump, which puts Mars, the Tower and fire on C, the Sun on D, Mercury, the Magician and air on E, Venus on F♯, the Moon, the High Priestess and water on G♯, Saturn on A and Jupiter on A♯. The letters and trumps are the Golden Dawn's, as the cards and the wheel have them. It is a correspondence, not an acoustics: nothing about a fifth makes Scorpio follow Aries, and the Pythagoreans, who had the planets sounding first, put them on other notes. The sky's chord takes where the seven stand this minute over Portland, from the wheel's ephemeris, rooted on the Sun's sign; the hour's key is the note of the planet whose hour it is, by the temple's clock of planetary hours. Your tunings, scales, chords and settings are kept in this browser and sent nowhere.

The hexagrams are China's, and older than the tone scale by two thousand years. Han scholars of the I Ching, Meng Xi among them, gave twelve of the sixty-four to the months: after the winter solstice the whole lines climb from the bottom, one a month, from Return to Heaven, all six whole, in the fourth month; after the summer solstice the broken ones climb the same way, from the Meeting to Earth. The Monthly Ordinances gave each month a pitch pipe too, the twelve lü, a semitone apart, from 黃鐘 Huángzhōng, the yellow bell, in the solstice's month; so each of the twelve hexagrams has a note. Where the yellow bell sits is the one choice left open, and the button decides it. Off, it is on C, as modern tables usually write it. On, it is on the note of the sign the winter solstice opens, Capricorn, which Case's scale makes A, so that the hexagrams go round the wheel with the year: the spring equinox's Great Strength falls on C with Aries, and the summer solstice's Meeting on D♯ with Cancer. The changes share the choice, and play a sovereign hexagram's note when one is cast.

The scales and chords are anyone's. The tone scale is Case's, in The Tarot (1947) and his Builders of the Adytum lessons; the trumps' letters and planets are Book T's. The neck, the shapes and the sound are made here, in one script with no library.
