Temple

On a far block of the city there is a temple, and this is the inside of it. The floor is the Ophite diagram, the one gnostic chart that survives, and only because Origen described it in order to argue with it. Round the floor stand sixteen signs, with the Monad in the middle: Abraxas and the serpent that eats its tail, the thirty aeons and the seven heavens, the Tree and the Tetraktys, a Mandaean standard and John Dee's figure of everything. Point at one and it comes to the middle, with what it means. Through the back of the nave is the library: a hundred-odd texts, gnostic, hermetic, kabbalistic, magical and mystical, and on three shelves at the back the conspiracies, the radicals and the machine, nearly every one kept here as a plain file to read in the reading room, every one older than copyright or given away by whoever wrote it, which are the only kinds a library like this can afford.

The Monad

The signs

Each is drawn from its description, as line art, the way the city and the backrooms are drawn; none is traced from a gem or a manuscript, so what you see is what the sources say and no more. The explanations are short and, as far as the sources allow, true; where a thing is disputed, it says so, and where everyone says a thing that is not so, it says that too.

The Monad

A circle with a point in it. The Pythagoreans' sign for the One, from which the numbers and then everything else proceed; the Hermetic books call the first thing Mind; the Valentinians called it Bythos, the Depth, and said nothing else about it, which was the point. Everything else in this room is a complication of this.

The Ouroboros

The serpent that eats its tail. It is drawn in the alchemical papyrus of Cleopatra, third-century Alexandria, round the words hen to pan, the all is one. The Ophites and the Pistis Sophia put it round the world as the outer darkness: the disc of the world sits inside the serpent's ring, and so do you. It is also the sign for a process that feeds on itself, which is how it came to be on the covers of chemistry books.

Abraxas

A cock's head, a man's body in armour, two serpents for legs, a whip in one hand and a shield in the other. Cut into thousands of gemstones in the second and third centuries, and named for the ruler of the 365 heavens in Basilides' system: the Greek letters of ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ add up to 365. Jung wrote seven sermons about him and signed them Basilides; Hesse put him in Demian. He is also where the word abracadabra does not come from, though everyone says so.

ΙΑΩ

The three vowels on the gems beside Abraxas: the Greek spelling of the name of the God of the Jews, which some gnostics took as the name of one archon among seven. Irenaeus lists them: Ialdabaoth, Iao, Sabaoth, Adonaios, Eloaios, Horaios, Astaphaios. The seven vowels ΑΕΗΙΟΥΩ ring it, one for each planet, chanted in order as the password past each heaven, which is why the magical papyri are full of lines that look like someone leaning on the keyboard.

Chnoubis

A serpent with a lion's head, rayed with seven or twelve rays, over a sign of three S's struck through. On the gems it is against pains of the stomach. In the Apocryphon of John the same figure is Ialdabaoth, the Demiurge, lion-faced and serpentine, who made this world without asking anyone and believes there is no god above him. He is wrong, which is the whole of gnosticism in a sentence.

The Ophite diagram

Celsus saw one around the year 178 and used it against the Christians; Origen answered him sixty years later with a better description of it; between them we have the only gnostic chart that survives, in words. Two circles in the middle, Father and Son, bound by a ring called Love; seven circles for the archons' heavens, each with its name and its password; and round everything the Leviathan, the world-serpent, the soul of all. It is the floor of this room. It is a map of what you have to climb out of.

The Pleroma

Valentinus' Fullness: thirty aeons in fifteen pairs, male and female, emanating from the Depth and Silence in an Ogdoad of eight, a Decad of ten and a Dodecad of twelve. The last and youngest is Sophia, Wisdom, who wanted to know the Depth by herself, could not, and whose grief, falling out of the circle, became the stuff of this world. Irenaeus wrote the whole system down to mock it, and thereby saved it.

Horos

The Limit, also called Stauros, the Cross: the boundary the Valentinians drew round the Pleroma to keep the Fullness in and the fall out. Sophia's intention is kept inside the ring; her passion is put outside it, where it becomes the Demiurge and the world we are in. The cross was a fence before it was anything else.

The Vesica Piscis

Two equal circles, each drawn through the other's centre, and the almond where they overlap. The shape of a fish, a seed, a gate; the first thing you draw in Euclid, and the first thing the cathedral builders drew for a window. The mystics put the Christ in it because it is where two worlds share an area without being the same world.

The Tetraktys

One, two, three, four, in a triangle: ten points. The Pythagoreans swore their oaths by it. It holds the point, the line, the plane and the solid, and the ratios of the octave, the fifth and the fourth, 2:1, 3:2, 4:3, so it is geometry and music at once, which was the claim. Mead's Hermes and the Kabbalists' Tree are both ways of counting to ten and meaning everything.

As above, so below

Two triangles, one up, one down, interlaced. The hexagram; the Seal of Solomon on the grimoires; the alchemists' fire and water; and the sentence from the Emerald Tablet that the whole Hermetic tradition rests on: that which is below is like that which is above, for the working of the one thing. The sentence is the method. The figure is the sentence, drawn.

The Tree

Ten sephiroth, the emanations of the Kabbalah, in three columns, joined by twenty-two paths, one for each Hebrew letter, as the Sefer Yetzirah counts them: thirty-two paths of wisdom in all. Kether at the top is the Crown; Malkuth at the bottom is this. It is the Pleroma's diagram, drawn by people who kept the books.

The Drabsha

A cross of two olive-wood staves, draped with a white cloth, with a myrtle wreath at the crossing: the standard of the Mandaeans of Iraq and Iran, the only gnostic religion still practised. It is not a crucifix. The cloth is the light, and the four arms are the four quarters of the world the light shines through. They baptise in running water on Sundays, hold John the Baptist for a prophet and Jesus for not one, and they are still here, which none of the rest of this room can say.

The Hieroglyphic Monad

John Dee's figure of 1564: a point in a circle for the Sun, a crescent above it for the Moon, a cross beneath for the four elements, and two arcs at the foot for Aries, fire. Dee claimed every sign of the planets and metals could be cut out of it and wrote the Monas Hieroglyphica to prove it in twenty-four theorems, which Elizabeth asked him to explain to her, and which nobody has entirely understood since, Dee included. The figure, constructed, and the theorems in plain words.

The pearl and the serpent

From the Hymn of the Pearl in the Acts of Thomas: a prince is sent from the East down into Egypt to fetch one pearl from the coils of a serpent, puts on the Egyptians' clothes, eats their food, forgets who he is, and sleeps; a letter from home, flying as an eagle, wakes him. The pearl is the soul, the serpent is the world, the letter is gnosis. It is the whole doctrine as a story a child could follow, which was the idea.

The Ogdoad

Seven arcs for the seven planets' heavens, which the archons keep, and an eighth beyond them, the sphere of the fixed stars, which is the first place that is free. The Hermetic Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth teaches the climb to it; the Valentinians' Sophia waits there for the rest of us. Above the seventh, in every version of the story, the rules change.

The tria prima

Sulphur, mercury and salt: Paracelsus' three principles, soul, spirit and body, in the signs the alchemists cut for them: a triangle on a cross, a circle crowned with a crescent on a cross, a circle with a bar through it. Mercury's sign is the one Dee cut his Monad from. Not elements, in their view, but what anything is made of in three different ways at once, and the reason a thing can burn, run and stay.

There is also a sigil press, for making your own: write a wish, it strikes the vowels and the repeats and binds the rest into one sign, you hold the sign to charge it, and when it is charged it burns. Spare's method, 1913, with the forgetting left to you. And a talisman press: Agrippa's seven planetary squares with their intelligences and spirits traced on them, and your own name traced by his Latin numeration, the hour's planet first. And the angelic alphabet: Dee's twenty-one letters from his own hand, a keyboard for them, and the nineteen Keys on the shelf; and the Great Table, the four Watchtowers rebuilt letter by letter from Dee's own book of the angels' names, to walk with the arrow keys.

The library

Every book on the first five shelves is in the public domain in the United States, and most of them everywhere: the translations are the Victorian and Edwardian ones, Mead's and Mathers' and Legge's and Budge's, made for the theosophists and the Golden Dawn and the Sacred Books of the East, and they are the reason the texts are free. Each link goes to a complete copy, at Project Gutenberg where it has one, otherwise at the Gnostic Society Library, the Esoteric Archives, New Advent, the Internet Archive, or the Internet Sacred Text Archive. Most are also kept here, as plain files, and open in the reading room, which remembers your place. The three shelves at the back are later and looser: the conspiracies, the radicals and the machine, out of copyright or given away by their authors, and the note says which. The shelf notes are mine.

Each entry is marked for what it is: ● kept here whole, as clean plain text · ◔ kept here as a scan's OCR, readable but rough · ◆ made here rather than kept · ○ a link out only · ☆ a place to start.

Where to start

A dozen, picked by hand, for anyone who does not know where to begin on a hundred-odd books:

  1. The Hymn of the Pearl the one gnostic text to read if you read one: a prince sent down to Egypt for a pearl, who forgets, and is written to. Ten minutes.
  2. Pistis Sophia the big gnostic book: Sophia falls out of the light and sings her way back, in Mead's whole translation, from the scan.
  3. The Emerald Tablet twelve lines in ten versions, from the Arabic to Newton's: as above, so below, and the rest.
  4. Thrice-Greatest Hermes Mead's Hermes, all three volumes: the Poimandres and the rest of the Corpus, with the commentary, from the scan.
  5. Monas Hieroglyphica not kept but drawn: the figure constructs itself from Dee's measure and the twenty-four theorems are put plainly.
  6. The Tao Teh King Legge's Tao, eighty-one short chapters; the one book here to read twice.
  7. The Book of the Damned the damned data, rains of frogs and stones and worse, kept by a clerk in the public library and set down deadpan.
  8. The Communist Manifesto a spectre is haunting Europe; thirty pages, and most of the rest of that shelf is arguing with it.
  9. The Gold-Bug where the backrooms' cipher comes from, with the key in it, and the rest of Poe's first volume behind it.
  10. The Jargon File the hackers' dictionary as it stood in 1996, the best self-portrait a culture has written of itself.
  11. The Conscience of a Hacker one page, 1986, written after an arrest, still quoted on walls.
  12. A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace 1996, wrong about nearly everything, and worth reading for the sound of it.

What is not here: the Nag Hammadi codices, found in a jar in 1945, whose English translations are younger than the copyright term; the Gnostic Society Library keeps them by arrangement. What the heresy-hunters quoted in order to refute is here, because they hated it carefully.

I. The gnostics

  1. ◐☆ Pistis Sophia Egypt, 3rd–4th c. · tr. G. R. S. Mead, 1921 · Gnostic Society Library · scan at the Internet Archive
    The longest gnostic text known before 1945: Jesus, eleven years after the resurrection, explaining the fall and repentance of Sophia, the thirteen aeons and the outer darkness, to the disciples, at length.
  2. ◐ Fragments of a Faith Forgotten G. R. S. Mead, 1900 · Gnostic Society Library · scan at the Internet Archive
    The whole of gnosticism as it could be known in 1900, school by school, from the fathers' quotations and the Coptic codices; still the best door in, and the one with the fewest footnotes.
  3. ◐☆ The Hymn of the Pearl Syria, 3rd c. · tr. G. R. S. Mead, 1908, as the Hymn of the Robe of Glory · Gnostic Society Library · scan at the Internet Archive
    The prince, the pearl, the serpent, the letter; a hundred and five lines, the one gnostic text everyone agrees is beautiful.
  4. ● The Acts of Thomas Syria, 3rd c. · tr. M. R. James, 1924 · Gnostic Society Library
    Thomas sold as a slave to an Indian king, building him a palace in heaven with the money; the Hymn of the Pearl is in the middle of it, sung in prison.
  5. ● The Gnostic Crucifixion from the Acts of John · G. R. S. Mead, 1907 · Gutenberg #35735
    The Cross of Light: Jesus explaining to John, on the Mount of Olives, during the crucifixion, that what is happening on Calvary is not what it looks like.
  6. ● The Gnôsis of the Light the Bruce Codex · tr. F. Lamplugh, 1918 · Gutenberg #30799
    The untitled treatise from the Bruce papyrus: the ineffable One and the emanations, in a Coptic that was translating a Greek that was straining. Short, and the deepest water on the shelf.
  7. ◐ Logia Iesou: Sayings of Our Lord Oxyrhynchus, 1897 · Grenfell & Hunt · Internet Archive
    One page of papyrus from a rubbish mound, with sayings nobody had read in sixteen centuries; forty-eight years later the Gospel of Thomas was found in Egypt and this turned out to be page one.
  8. ◐ The Gnostics and Their Remains C. W. King, 1887 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    The gems: Abraxas, Chnoubis, ΙΑΩ, drawn plate after plate by a man who collected them, with theories about them that are wrong in ways that are interesting.
  9. ● Forerunners and Rivals of Christianity Francis Legge, 1915 · vol. 2 · Gutenberg #71384
    Orphics, Essenes, Simon Magus, the Ophites, Valentinus, Marcion, Mithras, Mani and the Mandaeans, as the competition, by a man who read everything and liked none of it.
  10. ● Against Heresies Irenaeus of Lyons, c. 180 · tr. Roberts & Rambaut, 1885 · New Advent
    Book I is the Valentinian system, the thirty aeons, Sophia and Horos, the seven archons by name, set down by its enemy with a care that preserved it; the other four books are the refutation, which nobody reads.
  11. ● The Refutation of All Heresies Hippolytus of Rome, c. 225 · tr. F. Legge, 1921 · vol. 2 · Gutenberg #65478
    Thirty-three heresies traced to the Greek philosophers so that none could claim to be new: the Naassenes, the Peratae, the Sethians, Justin's Baruch, Basilides, all quoted at length to be refuted briefly.
  12. ● Against Celsus Origen, c. 248 · tr. F. Crombie, 1885 · New Advent
    Book VI, chapters 24 to 38: the Ophite diagram, the circles, the seven archons as animals, and the passwords for each gate, quoted by Origen to show that Celsus had confused the Ophites with the Church. The floor of this room.

II. The hermetica and the Platonists

  1. ○ The Corpus Hermeticum Egypt, 1st–3rd c. · tr. G. R. S. Mead, 1906
    The Poimandres, the Mind to Hermes, the Secret Sermon on the Mountain: the vision of the Shepherd of Men, the ascent through the seven spheres, the Ogdoad; what Ficino translated in 1463 and put the Renaissance up to.
  2. ◐☆ Thrice-Greatest Hermes G. R. S. Mead, 1906, three volumes · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    The whole of the Trismegistic literature with Mead's prolegomena, excerpts and commentary; the shelf's heaviest item, by weight and in every other way.
  3. ●☆ The Emerald Tablet Arabic, before 800 · several translations, Newton's among them · sacred-texts
    Thirteen sentences; as above, so below; the operation of the Sun. Newton translated it in his own hand, between the calculus and the Mint.
  4. ◐ The Virgin of the World Kore Kosmou, from Stobaeus · tr. Kingsford & Maitland, 1885 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    Isis to Horus on the making of souls and their complaint at being put in bodies; the Hermetic fragment that reads most like the gnostics, translated by two Victorian vegetarians.
  5. ● The Kybalion "Three Initiates", Chicago, 1908 · Gutenberg #14209
    Seven Hermetic principles, none of them in the Hermetica, by a man from the New Thought movement writing as three; the most-read Hermetic book of the twentieth century, and the least Hermetic. On the shelf for honesty.
  6. ● The Chaldaean Oracles of Zoroaster Julian the Theurgist, 2nd c. · ed. W. Wynn Westcott, 1895 · Esoteric Archives
    Fragments of a lost poem that the later Platonists treated as scripture; fire, the Father, the Hecate who is the World-Soul, and the warning not to stoop into the dark-gleaming world.
  7. ● The Golden Verses of Pythagoras tr. Fabre d'Olivet, 1813 · tr. N. L. Redfield, 1917 · Gutenberg #69174
    Seventy-one lines of rules for a Pythagorean life, with a French commentary ten times their length on the Tetraktys and everything else.
  8. ● On the Mysteries of the Egyptians, Chaldeans and Assyrians Iamblichus, c. 300 · tr. Thomas Taylor, 1821 · Gutenberg #72815
    The defence of theurgy, ritual as philosophy, by the Platonist who thought Plotinus had left too much out; Taylor's English is a period piece and so is the argument.
  9. ● The Enneads Plotinus, c. 270 · tr. K. S. Guthrie, 1918, four volumes · Gutenberg #42930
    The One, the Mind, the Soul, and the tractate Against the Gnostics, Ennead II.9, in which the greatest Platonist tells them, courteously, that they have made the world worse than it is.
  10. ● Timaeus Plato, c. 360 BC · tr. Benjamin Jowett, 1871 · Gutenberg #1572
    The craftsman who makes the world from a pattern, looking at it; the word Demiurge begins here, as a compliment, before the gnostics turned it round.

III. The kabbalah, the grimoires and the alchemists

  1. ◐ Sefer Yetzirah the Book of Formation, 3rd–6th c. · tr. W. Wynn Westcott, 1887 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    The ten numbers and the twenty-two letters as the thirty-two paths by which the world was engraved; six short chapters that the whole Tree is built on.
  2. ◐ The Kabbalah Unveiled from the Zohar · tr. S. L. MacGregor Mathers, 1887 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    The Book of Concealed Mystery and the Greater and Lesser Holy Assemblies, from Rosenroth's Latin, by the Golden Dawn's founder; the Ancient of Days described hair by hair.
  3. ● The Kabbalah: its Doctrines, Development and Literature Christian D. Ginsburg, 1865 · Gutenberg #69243
    The sober one: what the Kabbalah says and when it was written, by a scholar of the Hebrew Bible, before the occultists got hold of it.
  4. ○ Three Books of Occult Philosophy Cornelius Agrippa, 1533 · tr. "J. F.", 1651
    Natural, celestial and ceremonial magic, the whole Renaissance system in one place, the book every later grimoire copied from; with Joseph Peterson's notes, at the Esoteric Archives.
  5. ◐ The Key of Solomon the King Clavicula Salomonis, 14th–15th c. · tr. Mathers, 1889 · Esoteric Archives · scan at the Internet Archive
    The pentacles, the circle, the hours, the knife; the grimoire the others descend from, assembled by Mathers from seven manuscripts in the British Museum.
  6. ◐ The Lesser Key of Solomon Lemegeton, 17th c. · ed. Mathers & Crowley, 1904 · Esoteric Archives · scan at the Internet Archive
    Seventy-two spirits with their seals, ranks and specialities, the Goetia, and the four other books nobody mentions; Crowley's preface explains that the spirits are portions of the human brain, which he then summons.
  7. ◐ The Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses Germany, 1849 · New York, 1880 · Esoteric Archives · scan at the Internet Archive
    The grimoire of the Pennsylvania Dutch and the Caribbean, seals and all, which claims to be Moses' and is Stuttgart's; the most-owned magic book in America for a century.
  8. ● The Forty-Eight Keys, or Calls Dee and Kelley, 1584 · Casaubon, 1659 · as the Equinox printed them, 1912 · Casaubon at the Internet Archive · Internet Archive
    The nineteen Keys of the angelic language, delivered backwards a letter at a time at Cracow in 1584, in the angels' English and in the tongue itself; the nineteenth, with the thirty Aethyrs, is one Key thirty times. The alphabet is drawn next door.
  9. The Great Table of the Watchtowers Dee and Kelley, 1584, reformed 1587 · from the Tabula bonorum angelorum · drawn here
    The four tablets of twelve by thirteen letters and the black cross between them, rebuilt cell by cell from the names Dee drew out of them, the Kings, the Seniors, the Calvary crosses, the kerubs and the servients, so that every name reads back; walk it with the arrow keys and each cell says whose it is.
  10. ◆☆ Monas Hieroglyphica John Dee, Antwerp, 1564 · drawn here · the English in full
    Twenty-four theorems on the figure, dedicated to Maximilian II, written in twelve days. The page here draws the figure from Dee's own measure and gives each theorem in a few plain words with its figure; the full English is at the link, in copyright and not kept here.
  11. ◐ The Book of Enoch Ethiopia, 3rd–1st c. BC · tr. R. H. Charles, 1917 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    The Watchers who came down and taught metallurgy and cosmetics, the giants, the journeys through the heavens, the Son of Man; quoted in the Epistle of Jude and then lost to the West until 1773.
  12. ● The History of Magic Éliphas Lévi, 1860 · tr. A. E. Waite, 1913 · Gutenberg #70033
    The French ex-seminarian who invented modern occultism, on everyone from Zoroaster to Cagliostro, with the Baphomet drawing that every metal band has since borrowed.
  13. ● Hidden Symbolism of Alchemy and the Occult Arts Herbert Silberer, 1914 · tr. S. E. Jelliffe, 1917 · Gutenberg #27755
    A Freudian reads a Rosicrucian parable and finds the unconscious in it, five years before Jung did the same thing and got the credit.
  14. ● A New Light of Alchymie Michael Sendivogius, 1604, with Paracelsus on the tria prima · tr. John French, 1650 · Gutenberg #61112
    Twelve treatises on the philosophers' stone and a dialogue between Mercury and an alchemist in which Mercury is funnier; bound with the Paracelsian treatise on sulphur, mercury and salt.

IV. The mystics

  1. ◐ The Cloud of Unknowing England, late 14th c. · ed. Evelyn Underhill, 1922 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    An anonymous priest teaching a young man to put everything he knows under a cloud of forgetting and beat on the cloud above with a short word; the English via negativa, in English.
  2. ◐ The Works of Dionysius the Areopagite Syria, c. 500 · tr. John Parker, 1897 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    The Divine Names, the Mystical Theology, the two Hierarchies: the nine orders of angels and the God who is known by unknowing, by a writer who said he was St Paul's convert and was not.
  3. ● Dialogues on the Supersensual Life Jakob Böhme, 1622 · tr. William Law, 1764 · Gutenberg #33742
    The Görlitz shoemaker who saw the whole of things in the light on a pewter dish, in his plainest form: a master and a scholar talking about where the soul goes when it stops talking.
  4. ● Revelations of Divine Love Julian of Norwich, 1373–c. 1393 · ed. Grace Warrack, 1901 · Gutenberg #52958
    Sixteen showings to an anchoress in a cell in Norwich, the first book in English by a woman; a hazelnut in the palm of the hand, and all shall be well.
  5. ● Heaven and Its Wonders and Hell Emanuel Swedenborg, 1758 · tr. J. C. Ager, 1900 · Gutenberg #17368
    From things heard and seen: a Swedish mining engineer's report on the afterlife, in numbered paragraphs, with the geography, the languages and the marriages. Blake read it and argued with it for the rest of his life.
  6. ● The Confessions Augustine of Hippo, c. 400 · tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838 · Gutenberg #3296
    On the shelf for books III to V, the nine years he spent as a Manichaean hearer, the only inside account of a gnostic church by someone who left it and then ran the opposition.
  7. ● The Imitation of Christ Thomas à Kempis, c. 1420 · tr. William Benham, 1874 · Gutenberg #1653
    The other most-read Christian book: the inner life as a set of short sentences, by a monk who copied the Bible out four times by hand and had opinions about that.
  8. ● The Varieties of Religious Experience William James, 1902 · Gutenberg #621
    The Gifford Lectures: conversion, saintliness, mysticism, examined as experiences by a psychologist who took them seriously and believed almost none of them; the four marks of the mystical state are in lectures XVI and XVII.
  9. ● Practical Mysticism Evelyn Underhill, 1914 · Gutenberg #21774
    A little book for normal people, finished the week the war began, on how to do it: recollection, quiet, contemplation, in that order, no equipment.
  10. ● The Mesnevi Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī, 13th c. · tr. J. W. Redhouse, 1881, book I · Gutenberg #61724
    The reed cut from the reed-bed, complaining of separation: the first eighteen lines, and then the rest of book one, with the Acts of the Adepts in front of it. Whinfield's abridgement of all six books is on the sacred-texts shelf.
  11. ● Meditations Marcus Aurelius, c. 170 · tr. George Long, 1862 · Gutenberg #2680
    Not a mystic and not a gnostic, an emperor on campaign writing notes to himself; shelved here because he and Celsus and the Ophites were alive at the same time, and he was the one in charge.
  12. ● The Golden Ass Apuleius, c. 160 · tr. William Adlington, 1566 · Gutenberg #1666
    A man turned into a donkey; Cupid and Psyche in the middle; and in book XI, the only first-person account of an initiation into the mysteries, Isis', that antiquity left us.

V. The east, Egypt, and the theosophists

  1. ●☆ The Tao Teh King Laozi · tr. James Legge, 1891 · Gutenberg #216
    Eighty-one chapters on the way that cannot be spoken, spoken; the Victorian translation, which is wordier than the original by a factor of four and still shorter than this shelf note.
  2. ● Chuang Tzŭ: Mystic, Moralist and Social Reformer Zhuangzi, 4th c. BC · tr. Herbert Giles, 1889 · Gutenberg #59709
    The butterfly dream, the useless tree, the cook who never sharpens his knife; the funniest book on the shelf by a distance.
  3. ● The Song Celestial Bhagavad Gītā · tr. Edwin Arnold, 1885 · Gutenberg #2388
    Krishna to Arjuna on the field, between the armies, in blank verse; Gandhi's first Gita was this one.
  4. ◐ The Upanishads tr. F. Max Müller, 1879, Sacred Books of the East, vol. 1 · sacred-texts · scan at the Internet Archive
    Chāndogya, Kena, Aitareya, Kauṣītaki, Vājasaneyi: tat tvam asi, thou art that, in the first volume of the fifty that Müller edited and the one he translated himself.
  5. ● The Dhammapada Pali canon · tr. F. Max Müller, 1881 · Gutenberg #2017
    Four hundred and twenty-three verses on the path, beginning with the mind as the forerunner of all things; the Buddhist text most likely to be on a mug.
  6. ● The Book of the Dead the Papyrus of Ani, c. 1250 BC · tr. E. A. Wallis Budge, 1895 · Gutenberg #7145
    The spells for coming forth by day, the weighing of the heart, the negative confession; Budge's Egyptian is a century out of date and his English is why anyone cares.
  7. ● The Secret Doctrine H. P. Blavatsky, 1888, vol. 1 · 2, 3, 4 · Gutenberg #54824
    The Stanzas of Dzyan, from a book nobody else has seen, with fifteen hundred pages of commentary; the root of every root race, Atlantis and Lemuria, and the theosophy that paid for Mead's translations before he resigned from it.
  8. ● Isis Unveiled H. P. Blavatsky, 1877, vol. 1 · vol. 2 · Gutenberg #68705
    A master-key to the mysteries of ancient and modern science and theology, written in New York in two years by a woman who said the masters dictated it; the volume on theology has the gnostics in it, approvingly, and the Church, less so.
  9. ● Arguments of Celsus, Porphyry and the Emperor Julian against the Christians tr. Thomas Taylor, 1830 · Gutenberg #37696
    The other side, reassembled from the refutations: Celsus' True Word of 178, which only survives because Origen quoted nine-tenths of it in order to answer it. Shelved last, as a reminder of how any of this survived.

VI. The conspiracies, the hollow earth, and the damned

Not gnostic, but the same reflex: the world is not what it says it is, and somebody knows. Everything here is out of copyright, which is more than the conspiracies that came after it can say. The Anarchist Cookbook is in copyright and its recipes are wrong, which is the one thing everyone who has read it agrees on; it is not here.

  1. ● Proofs of a Conspiracy John Robison, 1797 · Gutenberg #47605
    Against all the religions and governments of Europe, carried on in the secret meetings of Free Masons, Illuminati and Reading Societies: the book the Illuminati panic comes from, by an Edinburgh professor of physics who believed every word, and Washington was sent a copy.
  2. ● Secret Societies and Subversive Movements Nesta H. Webster, 1924 · Gutenberg #19104
    The template for the twentieth century's conspiracy books, Illuminati to Bolsheviks in one line, and antisemitic at the root: she takes the Protocols for genuine. On the shelf as a specimen, the way a museum keeps a poison, so you can see where the later ones got it.
  3. ●☆ The Book of the Damned Charles Fort, 1919 · Gutenberg #22472
    Rains of frogs, black snow, things seen in the sky, every report science threw out, collected from the journals that threw them and set down with a deadpan nobody has matched; the word Fortean, and every later "unexplained."
  4. ● Atlantis: The Antediluvian World Ignatius Donnelly, 1882 · Gutenberg #4032
    A Minnesota congressman proves that Plato's island was real, was Eden, and taught the Egyptians and the Maya; the continent has never recovered. He also wrote Ragnarok, in which a comet did it.
  5. ● Symzonia: Voyage of Discovery "Captain Adam Seaborn", 1820 · Gutenberg #54485
    The first hollow-earth novel, written to sell John Cleves Symmes's theory that the planet is open at the poles and lived in; the inhabitants are better than us, which is the point of every hollow earth since.
  6. ● The Smoky God Willis George Emerson, 1908 · Gutenberg #3007
    A Norwegian fisherman sails through the polar opening, spends two years with the giants inside under a smoky inner sun, and tells the author on his deathbed; the hollow earth in its final form, with a map.
  7. ● Etidorhpa; or, The End of Earth John Uri Lloyd, 1895 · Gutenberg #37775
    A Cincinnati pharmacist's descent into the earth with an eyeless guide, part geology lecture, part temperance tract, part trip; the title is Aphrodite backwards, the illustrations are not, and the fungus chapter is the one people remember.
  8. ● The Mysterious Stranger Mark Twain, 1916 · Gutenberg #50109
    Satan's nephew visits an Austrian village in 1590, does miracles for the boys, and explains at the end that nothing exists, including the reader; Twain's last and least consoling book, put together after his death from three drafts.
  9. ● The Call of Cthulhu H. P. Lovecraft, 1928 · Gutenberg #68283
    A bas-relief, a cult in the bayou, a sunken city that rises for a day; out of copyright since 2024, so the Old One is now yours too. The most merciful thing in the world, it opens, is the inability of the human mind to correlate all its contents, which is this library's warning label.

VII. The radicals

The manifestos, the anarchists, and the dystopias that answered them, in the translations that are free. Industrial Society and Its Future, 1995, is in copyright like anything else written since 1929, its author's wishes notwithstanding, so it is a link and not a file.

  1. ●☆ The Communist Manifesto Marx and Engels, 1848 · tr. Samuel Moore, 1888 · Gutenberg #61
    A spectre is haunting Europe; twenty-three pages; the history of all hitherto existing society as the history of class struggles, and the workers with nothing to lose but their chains. The most-read political pamphlet ever written, and shorter than most of its footnotes.
  2. ● The Conquest of Bread Peter Kropotkin, 1892 · English, 1906 · Gutenberg #23428
    A prince, a geographer and an anarchist works out how a city feeds, houses and clothes itself with nobody in charge, with arithmetic; the book the Spanish anarchists carried in 1936.
  3. ● Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution Peter Kropotkin, 1902 · Gutenberg #4341
    Against the Social Darwinists: animals, savages, barbarians, the mediaeval city and ourselves survive by co-operating, not by the struggle of each against all; evolutionary biology has since come round, slowly, without saying so.
  4. ● God and the State Mikhail Bakunin, 1882 · Gutenberg #36568
    Unfinished, found among his papers: if God existed it would be necessary to abolish him; authority of every kind as the same thing in different hats, the priest, the king and the scientist.
  5. ● Anarchism and Other Essays Emma Goldman, 1910 · Gutenberg #2162
    Twelve essays by the most dangerous woman in America, the government's phrase: anarchism, minorities, prisons, the traffic in women, marriage and love, the drama; she was deported to Russia nine years later and did not like it there either.
  6. ● What Is Property? Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, 1840 · tr. Benjamin Tucker, 1876 · Gutenberg #360
    Theft, is the answer, on page one; the rest of the book is the argument, by the first man to call himself an anarchist, and Marx, who liked it, then wrote a book against him.
  7. ● The Ego and His Own Max Stirner, 1844 · tr. Steven Byington, 1907 · Gutenberg #34580
    Nothing is more to me than myself: the state, humanity, God, morality and the revolution as spooks in the head, dismissed one at a time by a Berlin schoolteacher who then went bankrupt in the milk trade.
  8. ● No Treason: The Constitution of No Authority Lysander Spooner, 1870 · Gutenberg #36145
    A Massachusetts lawyer argues that the Constitution binds nobody now alive, since none of us signed it, and that a government which claims otherwise is a band of robbers with better stationery; he had earlier run a private post office until Congress made it illegal.
  9. ● On the Duty of Civil Disobedience Henry David Thoreau, 1849 · Gutenberg #71
    One night in Concord jail for not paying the poll tax of a government that kept slaves and invaded Mexico, and the essay about it that Gandhi and King read; that government is best which governs not at all, when men are prepared for it.
  10. ● Catechism of a Revolutionist Sergey Nechayev, 1869 · marxists.org
    Twenty-six articles: the revolutionist is a doomed man, has no interests, no feelings, no property, and one passion; the pamphlet Dostoevsky wrote Demons about, after Nechayev murdered a comrade for doubting him. From marxists.org's transcription.
  11. ● Common Sense Thomas Paine, 1776 · The Age of Reason, 1794 · Gutenberg #147
    The pamphlet that turned a tax revolt into a revolution, anonymously, in plain English, and sold a copy to every fifth person in the colonies; and the later one against revealed religion, for which his country turned on him and six people came to his funeral.
  12. ● The Art of War Sun Tzu, c. 500 BC · tr. Lionel Giles, 1910 · Gutenberg #132
    Thirteen chapters on winning without fighting, with the Chinese commentators; the translation everyone quotes, by the British Museum's keeper of oriental books, who did it to correct a worse one.
  13. ● The Prince Niccolò Machiavelli, 1532 · tr. W. K. Marriott, 1908 · Gutenberg #1232
    How to get a state and keep it, written by a sacked civil servant to get his job back; it did not work, and his name became an adjective for the thing he described rather than did.
  14. ● We Yevgeny Zamyatin, 1921 · tr. Gregory Zilboorg, 1924 · Gutenberg #61963
    The One State, glass apartments, numbers for names, the Benefactor, the Green Wall and the operation that removes imagination; the first dystopia, banned in Russia before it was printed, and the one Orwell borrowed for 1984, which he admitted.
  15. ● R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots) Karel Čapek, 1920 · tr. Paul Selver, 1923 · Gutenberg #59112
    The play that gave the world the word robot, from the Czech for forced labour; the robots are organic, do all the work, and by act three have done the arithmetic.
  16. ● The Trial Franz Kafka, 1925 · tr. David Wyllie · Gutenberg #7849
    Someone must have slandered Josef K., for one morning, without having done anything wrong, he was arrested; the charge is never stated and the court is everywhere. The translator put his version in the public domain.
  17. ● Erewhon; or, Over the Range Samuel Butler, 1872 · Gutenberg #1906
    A country over the mountains where illness is a crime, crime is an illness, and the machines were destroyed five hundred years ago because they were evolving; the three chapters on that, The Book of the Machines, are the first argument about AI, made as a joke that has stopped being one.
  18. ● Looking Backward, 2000 to 1887 Edward Bellamy, 1888 · Gutenberg #624
    A Bostonian sleeps for 113 years and wakes in a socialist America with credit cards, which he invented the phrase for, and a system that works; the second-best-selling novel of its century in America, and clubs formed to bring it about.
  19. ● News from Nowhere William Morris, 1890 · Gutenberg #3261
    The answer to Bellamy from the man who designed the wallpaper: a London of the twenty-second century with no money, no state, no factories, and everyone making beautiful things slowly; the Houses of Parliament are used for storing manure.

VIII. The machine

The first program, the logic the machine runs on, the ciphers, and then the texts the machine's own people wrote and gave away: not public domain, these last, but free to copy by their authors' word, and the note on each says whose.

  1. ● Sketch of the Analytical Engine, with Notes by the Translator L. F. Menabrea, 1842 · tr. and annotated by Ada Lovelace, 1843 · Gutenberg #75107
    Menabrea's twenty pages on Babbage's unbuilt computer, and Lovelace's notes, three times as long, which contain the first published program (Note G, the Bernoulli numbers) and the first argument that such a machine might compose music and could originate nothing.
  2. ● Passages from the Life of a Philosopher Charles Babbage, 1864 · Gutenberg #57532
    The autobiography: the Difference Engine, the Analytical Engine, the government's money, the street musicians he sued, and the two occasions on which he was asked whether the machine would give the right answer if the wrong figures were put in.
  3. ○ An Investigation of the Laws of Thought George Boole, 1854
    Logic as algebra, with two values; the book every circuit in the machine you are reading this on is an application of, written by a self-taught Lincoln schoolmaster who died of walking to a lecture in the rain. Gutenberg has it as TeX and PDF only, so it is a link.
  4. ● Symbolic Logic Lewis Carroll, 1896 · Gutenberg #28696
    Part I, Elementary: syllogisms as a game with counters on a board, and the sorites puzzles about babies, crocodiles and illogical persons that are still set in logic courses because nobody has written better ones.
  5. ● Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions Edwin A. Abbott, 1884 · Gutenberg #201
    A square in a two-dimensional world is visited by a sphere, sees the third dimension, and is imprisoned for saying so; a satire on Victorian class that became the standard way of explaining the fourth.
  6. ●☆ The Gold-Bug, and the rest of volume one Edgar Allan Poe, 1843 · the Raven edition · Gutenberg #2147
    A cipher, a dead tree, a skull and Captain Kidd's gold: the story that taught America frequency analysis, by a man who had run a column breaking readers' ciphers and lost almost none. Volume one of the works, with the Rue Morgue and Hans Pfaall beside it. The key from the story is in use under this site, on a wall; the code clerk in the city will say where.
  7. ● Manual for the Solution of Military Ciphers Parker Hitt, 1916 · Gutenberg #48871
    The first American cryptanalysis textbook, by an infantry captain at Fort Leavenworth, with the frequency tables and the method for breaking the Playfair; the Army's codebreakers of 1917 learned from it.
  8. ● Elementary Cryptanalysis Helen Fouché Gaines, 1939 · Gutenberg #75074
    The book amateurs learned the trade from for fifty years, transposition and substitution and the Vigenère, with exercises; by the American Cryptogram Association's "Piccola", and public domain because nobody renewed it.
  9. ● The Devil's Dictionary Ambrose Bierce, 1911 · Gutenberg #972
    Cynic, n.: a blackguard whose faulty vision sees things as they are, not as they ought to be. On this shelf for the definitions of logic, telephone, and telegraph, and because this site's voice owes him.
  10. ● The Hacker Crackdown Bruce Sterling, 1992 · Gutenberg #101
    Law and disorder on the electronic frontier: the 1990 raids, Operation Sundevil, the Legion of Doom, the E911 document, Steve Jackson Games, and the founding of the EFF, by a novelist who was there; he gave it to the net himself in 1994, which is why it is on Gutenberg and here.
  11. ● Underground Suelette Dreyfus, with Julian Assange, 1997 · Gutenberg #4686
    Hacking, madness and obsession on the electronic frontier: the Australian and British hackers of the late eighties, Mendax among them, told from inside; the authors released it free in 2001.
  12. ●☆ The Jargon File 1975 onward · version 4.4.7, 2003, ed. Eric S. Raymond · catb.org
    The hacker's dictionary, begun at Stanford and MIT on the ARPANET, grown for thirty years: foo, bogon, the Jargon of the Lisp machine and the PDP-10, and the appendix on how to spot one. Placed in the public domain by its editor.
  13. ● The GNU Manifesto Richard Stallman, 1985 · gnu.org
    Why he was writing a free Unix and would give it away, with answers to the objections, most of which were then made; the document the free software movement, and so most of the internet's plumbing, descends from. Verbatim copying permitted, it says, and so it is.
  14. ●☆ A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace John Perry Barlow, Davos, 1996 · the EFF
    Governments of the Industrial World, you weary giants of flesh and steel: written in a night at the World Economic Forum in answer to the Telecommunications Act, by a Grateful Dead lyricist, and wrong about nearly everything in a way that still reads as right. From the EFF, which publishes it freely.
  15. ● RFC 1149: IP over Avian Carriers D. Waitzman, 1 April 1990 · the RFC Editor
    The internet's standards body publishes a standard for sending packets by pigeon; it was implemented in Bergen in 2001, nine packets sent, four received, a 55% loss and a round-trip time of about an hour. RFCs are free to copy.
  16. ● RFC 1925: The Twelve Networking Truths R. Callon, 1 April 1996 · the RFC Editor
    The first truth is that it has to work; the eleventh, that every old idea will be proposed again with a different name and a different presentation, regardless of whether it works; the twelfth, that perfection is reached not when there is nothing left to add but when there is nothing left to take away. Also free to copy.
  17. ●☆ The Conscience of a Hacker The Mentor, Phrack 7, 1986 · Phrack
    The Hacker Manifesto: written after an arrest, a page long, read aloud in the film, pinned to a thousand walls; yes, I am a criminal, my crime is that of curiosity. Phrack is free to redistribute and the author has said so of this.

Drawn on a canvas, no library, in one script; the signs and the shelves are plain HTML and read the same without it. The explanations are short and mine; the sources are the shelf, and the shelf is where to disagree with them. The temple is a block in the city, where the hierophant will ask whether you have been.
