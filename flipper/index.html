Apps for the Flipper Zero: a 128×64 screen, a d-pad and a buzzer.

Hanzi Cards study

Mandarin flashcards: simplified or traditional characters, pinyin with tone marks, and the tones played on the speaker.

  • HSK 1 to 4, and four small decks. 150, 156, 292 and 601 words, plus Numbers and time, Food, Travel and Measure words, each deck with its own progress. HSK 1 and 2 come in a rough learning order; HSK 3 and 4 are shuffled.
  • Simplified or traditional, switchable at any time, with the characters drawn from Noto Sans CJK.
  • You hear the tones. When a card flips, the speaker glides through each syllable's tone contour - high and flat, rising, dipping, falling - so you hear the shape of the word.
  • A tone quiz. Instead of flipping the card, pick each syllable's tone on the d-pad: up for the first, right for the second, down for the third, left for the fourth. The answer shows any wrong syllables inverted, and the card only counts as Good if every tone was right.
  • Spaced over days. A card you know comes back after 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 and then 60 days, and one you miss comes back the same day until you get it, with a daily limit on new cards. A stats screen shows what's due, today's reviews and your streak, and you can browse a whole deck without touching your progress. Progress is saved to the SD card.

Step Seq music

A step sequencer for the Flipper's piezo speaker, with a melody grid and a drum row. The speaker can only play one tone at a time, so a drum takes the first few milliseconds of its step and the note gets the rest.

  • Always in key. One note per step across 15 pitch rows that follow the scale you pick: minor or major pentatonic, minor, major, blues or chromatic, in any root.
  • Notes that do more than beep. A note can be short or held for up to eight steps, and an accent plays it a level louder. A chord is played chiptune-style, cycling fast through its notes, since the speaker has one voice: the octave, the fifth, or a triad from the scale.
  • Drums from square waves and noise. A kick, snare, hi-hat, open hat, clap and tom on the bottom row.
  • Eight patterns and a song. Each pattern is 16 or 32 steps with its own tempo (60 to 240 bpm), swing, scale, root and octave, and a song chains up to 16 of them. Transpose, shift, a random melody and undo help build variations, and everything is saved to the SD card when you quit.
  • Export writes the melody as a file the Flipper's own Music Player can play.

NW Crawl game

A tiny roguelike set on the alphabet streets of NW Portland. Start on Burnside and head north one street per floor - Couch, Davis, Everett, Flanders, all the way to Thurman - until you reach the Witch's Castle in Forest Park.

  • Nineteen streets and a castle. Each floor is a randomly generated maze of rooms, corridors and side alleys under fog of war: wide open blocks in the south, long industrial corridors further north, and the trees of Forest Park at the end.
  • The locals. Rats bite, crows fly over walls, raccoons steal a coffee and run, rogue e-scooters charge down a straight line and coyotes hunt in pairs. On the northern streets a Sasquatch lurks by the exit, invisible until he's two tiles away.
  • An umbrella and a slingshot. The umbrella, found on Davis, shoves a local two tiles back and stuns it. Beat Sasquatch for his slingshot, which fires pebbles up to five tiles.
  • Coffee, donuts and IPAs. Coffee heals, a pink-box donut raises your max HP, a hazy IPA raises your attack and a used book shows you the floor.
  • Landmarks. Powell's Books on Couch, the food carts on Kearney, the streetcar on Lovejoy, and Joe's Cellar on Pettygrove, where the bartender pours you one stiff drink.
  • Saved at every street. Quit and pick the walk up from the start of the street you reached. The title screen keeps your furthest street, your wins and your fastest win.
  • The Witch hexes you from range, summons crows and vanishes when hit.

Yulia's Cats pet

A cozy virtual pet. Yulia shares a little room with her two cats: Nugget, an elderly tiger-striped gentleman with white socks, and Baby, a chonky grey cat with big bright eyes.

  • Feed, play and pet. Dinner for both, a treat each, yarn to chase, or the laser dot, which Baby cannot resist and Nugget cannot be bothered with. Pet one and the Flipper purrs.
  • The cats get on with their day. Nugget watches the birds from the window sill and keeps an eye on the food dish; Baby naps in the patch of afternoon sun, follows Nugget around and nudges him off his spot by the dish.
  • The room follows the clock. The sun crosses the window, the sky goes dark in the evening and the lamp comes on, and late at night the cats get sleepy on their own.
  • Yulia speaks Russian to the cats now and then, with the English underneath. She also reads, makes art and plays her keyboard through the speaker.
  • She reads and draws. While she reads, the page shows a line or two from an old favourite (Austen, Carroll, Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Mary Shelley, Confucius, Lao Tsu, Kipling's cat who walks by himself, and more), and the title comes up when she closes the book. When she finishes a piece of art she holds it up full screen: portraits of the cats, the two of them, the view from the window, a pair of giraffes, five abstracts after Malevich, Kandinsky and Mondrian, and seven studies drawn fresh each time, among them a fractal cat. A sketchbook keeps everything she has drawn.
  • She dreams. A moment after the lights go out at nap time Yulia drifts off, and a dream plays on the screen: Nugget the size of a hill, a rain of fish for Baby, a night train through the mountains and a gondola in Venice with her tall curly-haired fellow, a dragon ride over a castle (the dragon is Nugget with wings), the cats on the moon, a tunnel of rings with a great cat's eye at the end, and now and then a nightmare, such as a vacuum cleaner the size of a bus. Fourteen in all, each written up in her dream journal, where it can be played again.
  • Make the room hers. A wardrobe of nine hair styles in three colours, eight pairs of glasses (or none) and nine sweaters; seven rugs, a shelf plant, a cat tree and fairy lights; a record player in six genres, which everyone reacts to; and a photo album of fifteen milestones to fill, three of them for reading every book, filling the sketchbook and filling the dream journal.
  • Nothing bad ever happens. Time passes while the app is closed, so the cats get hungry and want to play, but that's all.

Tricorder tools

A handheld signal scanner, styled after a certain starship's field instrument. Eight sensors, and one sweep that runs them all. It logs the stardate when it starts.

  • RF. A Sub-GHz spectrum sweep with peak hold, or a scrolling waterfall. Lock on to the peak to chart its strength over time, hear it as Geiger clicks that quicken as the signal grows, and name any protocol the firmware recognises. It only listens.
  • IR. The last infrared signal as a waveform, with its protocol, address and command. Send it back out, or save it to the SD card for the Flipper's own Infrared app.
  • NFC, RFID and iButton. Whether a reader field is nearby, which NFC protocols a tag answers to, and 125 kHz fobs, cards and iButton keys.
  • A probe and the battery. A GPIO pin's voltage (0 to 2.5 V) as a number and a trace, and the battery's voltage, temperature, charge and current draw.
  • The full sweep. RF, IR, EMF and TAG in turn, under a progress bar with a notch for each, and a chirp as each sensor takes over. Every pass ends with a report card.
  • It announces a contact. A new one on any sensor flashes the panel, buzzes and blinks the LED, and it warbles, higher for stronger signals. Between contacts a dim LED breathes in the colour of the sensor that's scanning.

Jiggler usb

A mouse jiggler that keeps a computer awake without getting in your way. The Flipper plugs in over USB as a mouse.

  • Invisible moves. Each move is a 1 px nudge and an immediate nudge back, so the computer's idle timer resets but the pointer never visibly leaves its spot. It can also wiggle, trace a tiny square, or tap F15 or Shift for programs that only watch the keyboard.
  • Random timing. Set a shortest and a longest wait, from 5 seconds to 10 minutes, and each move comes at a random moment in between.
  • It pauses while you work. A small desktop helper tells the Flipper how long the computer has been idle and whether the screen is locked, over a USB interface of its own, so it needs no special permissions. The jiggler holds off until you've been idle a while. Without the helper it still works, it just can't tell when you're busy.
  • Run timers. Stop after 15 minutes to 12 hours, or at a time of day from the Flipper's clock.
  • Status at a glance. What it's doing, when the next move is due, how many moves it has made, how long it has been running and what the helper reports. Settings are saved to the SD card.

Spectre toy

A ghost hunting kit. Four tabs share one “spirit energy” level, fed by the real sensors where the hardware has them and by the hardware random number generator for the rest. It is a toy, and says so.

  • SENSE. A needle from QUIET to CONTACT with Geiger crackle that quickens as it climbs. A nearby NFC or 125 kHz reader field pushes it hard, and a drop in the battery temperature since you started reads as a cold spot.
  • BOX. A spirit box: it sweeps a Sub-GHz band in 64 steps, draws the noise floor and plays the channel hopping as static. When a frequency carries a signal it does not usually have, it locks on, the tone wavers, and a word comes through. It only listens.
  • VOICE. Words arrive on their own when the energy surges, spelled out letter by letter with a garbled syllable each. OK asks a question and waits for the answer. The last three words stay as a transcript.
  • RADAR. A sweeping scope with echoes that drift, the nearest one's range and bearing, a ping as the beam crosses each, and a yes or no on OK.
  • Every surge flashes the screen, buzzes, and the LED breathes in the tab's colour, brighter the more excited the kit gets. Hold OK to mute.

Grimoire temple

The temple's spells, off the browser and onto the device: a pendulum, a sigil, a talisman and a wand, with the planetary hour on every page. Built from issue 147.

  • Pendulum. Hold a question in mind and press OK. The bob wanders, then settles: to and fro is yes, side to side is no, round and round means ask again. The answer comes from the hardware random source.
  • Sigil. Type a wish; the vowels and the repeats are struck and what is left is bound into one sign after Spare. It is the sigil press's engine ported step for step, checked against it to four decimals, so a wish gives the same sigil here as there. Hold OK four seconds to charge it; it burns, and nothing is kept.
  • Talisman. Agrippa's square of the hour's planet, from the talisman press's data, with its intelligence traced solid, its spirit dotted, and a typed name in a thick line by his Latin numeration.
  • Wand. A short Sub-GHz blessing carrying the planetary hour, cast on OK. A second Flipper on the same page hears it, shows whose hour it was cast in, and answers. It transmits only then, and only where the region allows.
  • Hours. The planetary clock, computed on the device from the Flipper's clock and Portland's sunrise with the same rule as the site's, in every footer and as a table of today's day and night.

Portal Putt game

Minigolf on the Flipper: nine holes that take turns between fantasy and sci-fi, from Goblin Green and the Wizard's Spiral to Docking Bay 9, Black Hole Bend and the Rift. Par is 29.

  • The putt. Left and right turn the aim, and holding keeps it turning; up and down nudge it a quarter step. Hold OK and a meter rises and falls; let go to putt. Back pauses.
  • Portals. They come in pairs: in one, out the other, still rolling.
  • Gravity wells. They pull harder the closer the ball gets, and touch the centre and the ball is gone.
  • Lava and the void. A stroke's penalty, and the ball goes back to where the shot started.
  • And the rest. Conveyors carry the ball, slime and moon dust hold it back four times as hard, bumpers send it off faster than it came, and some walls slide on a cycle of their own.
  • The card. A hole stops at par plus four. Your best round is kept on the SD card, and the app transmits nothing. A solver on the host checks that every hole can be sunk within par.
